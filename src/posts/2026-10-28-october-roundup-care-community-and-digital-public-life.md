What does care look like when our relationships, scholarship, congregations, and infrastructure become part of digital public life? Recent contributions to Public Theology approach that question from several directions. Here is a reading roundup, with an invitation to spend time with the full essays.

## More than a Dot on a Map

In [“More than a Dot on a Map: Network, Care, and Public Theology”](/more-than-a-dot-on-a-map/), **James E. Jones Jr.** considers how network analysis might help a congregation recognize gaps in care. His proposed sketch links pastoral leadership, deacons, volunteers, greeters, and households without exposing private needs. A visible connection can help someone receive support; it cannot measure a person's worth. Consent, confidentiality, and the ability to correct a representation belong at the center of the work.

## Digitization Is Never Just a Click

**James E. Jones Jr.** also reflects on the people and decisions behind digital scholarship in [“Digitization Is Never Just a Click”](/digitization-is-never-just-a-click/). Selecting materials, describing them, securing permissions, and making them accessible are acts of interpretation and labor. Community members deserve a part in telling their own stories, and accessibility belongs in the work from the beginning. Clear headings, alternative text, readable pages, and transcripts make the digital table more welcoming.

## Between the Parade and the Temple

In [“Between the parade and the temple”](/between-the-parade-and-the-temple/), **J. Michael Mills** distinguishes change that happens to a congregation from transition that people build together. Thinking of churches as networks of trust opens questions about neighbors, agency, and belonging. Inclusion asks more than who appears on the map: it asks who can lead, whose relationships are recognized, and how trust can grow.

## A Data Center Should Earn Its Place

Our introduction to **Matthew Lingafelt's** *Altoona Mirror* op-ed, [“Data center should earn its place”](/data-center-should-earn-its-place/), brings public theology into a local infrastructure debate. At a Taylor Township hearing, Lingafelt asks what the community stands to gain from a proposed data center. Community benefits agreements, electricity costs, noise, and air quality are questions about what neighbors owe one another as well as questions of development policy. The post links to his full op-ed.

Across these pieces, a common question returns: who benefits, who participates, and who might be left out? We invite you to read, share, and bring those questions into your own communities.
