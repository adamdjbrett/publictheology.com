Whose public do we mean when we say public theology? Which theology? I will argue that public theology is theology done in public, for the public, and answerable to the public; that it matters now because the public itself is in trouble; and that it is at its best when it is inclusive, interreligious, and ecumenical. We can either lament what lacks or we can create what we wish to see in the world. Therefore, I will also make the case for [publictheology.com](https://publictheology.com) and [Ebenezer: A Journal of Public Theology](https://journal.publictheology.com) as a digital public spheres.

## What is public theology?

The term has a birthday. Martin Marty coined "public theology" in 1974 to distinguish the work of theologians from the "civil religion" Robert Bellah had described, a national faith that borrowed religious symbols while belonging to no tradition (Kim and Day, *A Companion to Public Theology*, 2017). David Tracy gave the term its structure in *The Analogical Imagination* (1981): every theologian addresses three publics, the academy, the church, and the wider society. Most theology speaks to the first two and forgets the third; public theology remembers.

E. Harold Breitenberg Jr., surveying the field in 2003, found enough consensus to offer a working definition: "theologically informed public discourse about public issues, addressed to the church, synagogue, mosque, temple or other religious body, as well as the larger public or publics, argued in ways that can be evaluated and judged by publicly available warrants and criteria." I take that definition as my own, with one gloss. Ronald Thiemann (*Constructing a Public Theology*, 1991) worried that Tracy's demand for warrants acceptable to "all rational persons" would strip theology of its particular convictions before it ever reached the square. Following Thiemann, I argue that public theology does not translate itself into neutral speech; it speaks from a tradition, in terms a neighbor can contest.

## Why it matters now

Public theology matters today because the public is in trouble, and theology is implicated in the trouble. Christian nationalism in the United States is a public theology; so is the Doctrine of Christian Discovery, which still sits in federal Indian law (*Johnson v. M'Intosh*, 1823, remains good law) and still shapes who counts as a people with rights to land. Theology never left public life; it stopped being argued there and started being asserted. The lacuna is not between religion and politics; the lacuna is between assertion and argument.

When theology is only asserted, the public shrinks to whoever already agrees. When theology is argued, with reasons that can be weighed by those outside the tradition, the public widens. Public theology is the practice of widening. Kim and Day describe public theology as "theology for others and done with others," and they name dialogue (self-critique, transparency, accountability) as one of its defining marks. A theology of domination fails every one of those tests, and secular argument alone will not answer it. It must be answered theologically, in the open, by people who know the texts.

## At its best: inclusive, interreligious, ecumenical

Public theology is at its best when it is inclusive, interreligious, and ecumenical, and the three terms are not synonyms.

Inclusive means that the "public" in public theology is the whole public, not the churched public. Nancy Fraser's critique of Habermas ("Rethinking the Public Sphere," 1990) applies with full force: a single bourgeois public sphere has always excluded women, the enslaved, the colonized, and the poor, and the excluded have built counterpublics of their own. Benjamín Valentín (*Mapping Public Theology*, 2002) takes up Fraser's "subaltern counterpublic" to connect Latino/a and liberation theologies to a broader public project; Rosemary Carbine and Jennifer Baldwin do similar work from feminist and activist locations. A public theology that cannot hear the counterpublics is a parochial theology with good branding.

Interreligious means that the public is multireligious, and the theology must be too. Breitenberg's definition already names the synagogue, mosque, and temple. The questions that matter (land, water, war, the dignity of persons, what we owe the dead and the unborn) are not Christian questions. They are human questions on which Haudenosaunee thinkers, Muslim jurists, Jewish ethicists, and Buddhist teachers have said things the rest of us need to hear. A cosmovision (Philip P. Arnold's term) is a whole account of how the world hangs together, and one tradition's account is never the only one in the room.

Ecumenical means that within Christianity the conversation crosses the lines denominations draw. The *T&T Clark Handbook of Public Theology* (2022) devotes a full section to "the ecumenical scope of public theology" because the Catholic, the Lutheran, the Baptist, and the Pentecostal are arguing about the same public even when they are not talking to each other. A public theology that speaks for one confession is a confessional theology that has wandered outdoors.

## Capacious and creative

Against those who would police the field, I affirm that public theology is capacious and creative. Hübenthal and Alpers observe that since 1974 the discipline has moved in a "centrifugal rather than centripetal direction." Good. Capacious means there is room for the systematic theologian and the organizer, the legal scholar and the poet, the preacher and the podcaster. The form of the work is not what makes it public theology; the address is. Creative means the public changes and the theology must keep up. A public theology of 1974 took the newspaper and the pulpit as its venues; a public theology of 2026 must take the feed, the stream, and the group chat as seriously as any pulpit. In "America," popular culture is not an illustration of public theology; it is where most public theology happens, and the scholar who ignores it ignores the primary source.

## Holding space for a digital public sphere

Jürgen Habermas, in *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere* (1962; English 1989), located the modern public sphere in the coffeehouses, salons, and periodicals of the eighteenth century, where private persons reasoned together and where, at least in principle, the better argument rather than the higher rank carried the day. Habermas was writing an elegy; he thought mass media had colonized that sphere. He was not wrong, and he could not have imagined what came next.

[publictheology.com](https://publictheology.com) and [Ebenezer: A Journal of Public Theology](https://journal.publictheology.com) are an attempt to hold space for a digital coffeehouse, with Fraser's corrections built in. The blog is the periodical: short, timely, argued, open to reply. The journal is the slower conversation: peer reviewed, open access, and committed to the idea that argument about public life should itself be public rather than paywalled. Neither is a pulpit. Both are tables. The criterion for a seat is not confession but the willingness to give reasons and to hear them. That is a modest ambition and, at present, a rare one.

Whose public? Everyone's. Which theology? Those commited to love, justice, inclusion, and the work of the prophetic. Gather round.
