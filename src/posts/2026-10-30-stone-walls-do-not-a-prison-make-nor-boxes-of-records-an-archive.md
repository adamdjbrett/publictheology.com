Every pastor in the Evangelical Lutheran Church in America has the responsibility to maintain the parish register. That record contains all of the pastoral acts that happen within a congregation. This record is a legal document and there are stories of people using the parish register to prove their existence, or at least some detail like their birth date or their family connection. Theoretically, the entire voting membership could be reconstructed using the parish register. For many Lutheran congregations this book is identical, a reddish-brown ledger broken into sections listing every baptism, confirmation, wedding, funeral and transfers in and out. To be honest, more and more congregations, despite their denominational affiliation are moving to a digital system of record keeping. In my first call, I would occasionally receive a phone call asking about a baptismal or confirmation date. I would open those large ledger pages and comb through entries to find the requested date.

The book however is not infinite. It does from time to time become full. Congregations that have been around for a long time understand that at some point they will need to get a new one. But what to do about the register that is now full? There is a need for an archive. Our synod (the regional grouping of congregations, akin to the dioceses of the Roman Catholic and Episcopal churches) supports a tri-synod archive. Northwestern and Southwestern Pennsylvania synods, along with the West Virginia-Western Maryland synod, share an archive on the campus of Thiel College.

These synods have recently pledged increased support to hiring an actual archivist to organize something like two hundred or so years of information from many sources rather than just boxes stacked in something resembling an order. Serving on synod council that authorized the expenditure of the funds to pay for the archivist, there was little debate about the theoretical underpinning of the archives. At the moment, creating order in the chaos of boxes of records, official or otherwise, seemed the best idea. After all, if pastors are required to care for the record, some other individual or entity should care for these records in the long run.

A mass of mouldering boxes in some distant college basement does not an archive make. This collection is a fortress of silence. No answers are easily forthcoming. People are left with assumptions that may or may not be born out when looking at actual data. Silence keeps stories from being told. The cost of creating and maintaining such an archive seems expensive, but the impermeable silence is more costly. The stories that get swallowed up keep us from seeing a true picture of the congregations of our area. Who belonged?  We are denied seeing the ways congregations responded to the needs of the communities in which they were situated. Every congregational budget is a narrative after all. Silence only keeps us in our own assumptions.

Good archives provide a window and a means to peer through the window. In [The Valley of the Shadow](https://valley.newamericanhistory.org/), the [New American History](https://www.newamericanhistory.org/) project provides an incredible look at two counties separated by about two hundred miles. Augusta County in Virginia and Franklin County in Pennsylvania have detailed records from 1859 through 1870, before, during and after the Civil War. The archive contains searchable tax records, letters and diaries, church records, and much more. It has timelines comparing the two counties. This archive provides a place where the stories of these counties can be explored. A drawback to this archive, however, is the reliance on written works. The stories of those who could not write, or write at such a level, are lost.

These archives are incredibly important, and perhaps even more important is to get our archives in good order as more and more congregations move to computerized records. How do we keep track of records when a software package goes away? Or a version shift leaves the parish register locked behind an encryption that people cannot get to? Mouldering boxes would be better, by far. Commitment to reliable archives is vital.
