The summer after I completed my undergraduate degree, when it was becoming clear that graduate school was where God was urging me to go, there were two different types of programs I was considering. Seminary was the one that won out, as I went on to get an MDiv and then an MTS at Garrett-Evangelical Theological Seminary, before beginning the PhD program at United Lutheran Seminary this academic year. However, a masters in library science with a focus in archival studies was my very close second choice, and where I likely would have wound up if God hadn't called me to the seminary. 

My love of archives started in undergrad, when I worked in the archives of my university library, processing and cataloguing new (well, old but new to us) materials. Last month, I found myself in the archive once more, again as a student worker and again processing and cataloguing materials, this time at my current home institution of ULS. To me, there is something deeply theological about the archive, whether that archive is part of a theological institution or a secular one. As I hold an artefact from someone whose bodily presence is gone from this world, I hold a part of their story, getting a glimpse into a piece of their being and what they left behind. The dust and dirt left on materials in the archive is a reminder that our existence is a corporeal one as well as a spiritual one. The work of an archivist and the work of a theologian take part in the sacred sharing of human stories, stories that have not always been told equitably. Archives that focus on materials from marginalized groups like the queer community and others often suffer from issues of “funding, staffing, and space.”[^1] 

My own research focuses on theologies and lived experiences of those who remain marginalized even within the ever-growing field of queer theology. This includes, but is not limited to, the BDSM community, non-monogamous communities, and sex workers. As with sex and theology, sex and the archive have a complicated history. Pornographic material is a significant part of cultural storytelling, and is key to understanding a culture's attitudes toward sex and its intersection with power. It produces information that “may be historical, sexological, erotic, or forensic.”[^2] Pornography has a rich history, with sexually explicit artwork even being found in the ruins of Pompeii—and then, although preserved by archivists, kept concealed away from other archived materials of Pompeii’s ruins due to their provocative nature.[^3] 

Much of the important work of preserving and archiving porn has been done by individual collectors working out of their homes, rather than professional archivists. These home-brewed archives are vital to the preserving of cultural materials, especially those like porn with a long history of being kept out of the archive due to stigma, but they run the risk of  a person’s ++[“life’s work end[ing] up in a garbage dumpster.”](https://www.vice.com/en/article/why-libraries-need-to-archive-porn/)++ Increased access to digitization has been helpful for long-term preservation of pornographic materials. On the ++[BDSM Art Archive](https://bdsmartarchive.com/)++ and her various other sites, for example, art curator Karen Smits has made hundreds of pieces of BDSM art and photography viewable for free, where these materials may otherwise have been lost to history.

In my PhD coursework these past couple weeks, I have been developing an Omeka site curating a small exhibit from the works of Studio Biederer. As Smits also explains on one of her sites, verifiable by other historical records, ++[Studio Biederer was a French BDSM photography/film studio run by Jewish brothers Jacques and Charles Biederer in the 1920s and ‘30s, before the brothers were killed in the Holocaust in the 1940s.](https://studiobiederer.wordpress.com/biederer-studio/)++ As of this post’s publication, ++[my Omeka site](https://artofkink.omeka.net/)++ is still a work in progress, but from it I hope to inspire the viewer to consider the joy and mutuality of erotic connection that often goes overlooked by anti-pornography sentiments, evidence of which is easily lost if pornography is kept out of the archive.
