One important insight I took from Smith and Wheatley’s discussion of the labor of digitization is that digital projects do not simply appear because someone presses a button or scans a document. Digitization is human labor. Someone must select the material, prepare it, scan or transcribe it, describe it through metadata, correct errors, secure permissions, and determine how it will be presented to the public. What appears effortless on a screen may represent hours of intellectual, emotional, and technical work behind the scenes. The digital object, therefore, is never completely neutral. It bears the marks of the people, institutions, resources, and decisions that brought it into view.

This insight connects powerfully with Tuskegee Literary Legacies. The project demonstrates that community-engaged public humanities should not merely extract historical materials from a community and place them online. It should invite communities to participate in interpreting, preserving, and sharing their own stories. Tuskegee is not simply a location from which information is collected; it is a living community whose members carry memory, knowledge, and interpretive authority. That distinction matters. A responsible digital project must ask not only, “What can we digitize?” but also, “Whose story is this, who should help tell it, and who benefits from its circulation?”

As I began thinking about and writing my [Humanities Commons site](https://jejones.hcommons.org/), I was surprised by how many decisions were hidden inside what seemed like a basic assignment. Choosing a title, organizing pages, selecting images, writing descriptions, and imagining an audience are all interpretive acts. What initially felt inaccessible was the technical language surrounding HTML, CSS, themes, blocks, and web accessibility. Yet I am learning that I do not have to become a professional web developer to create a meaningful digital space. I need to understand enough to make thoughtful and ethical choices.

I imagine several audiences reading my site: scholars interested in public theology, students encountering my teaching, clergy seeking practical resources, members of the Black Church, and residents of the communities in which I serve. My portfolio should speak to all of them without assuming everyone has the same academic vocabulary, technological access, or physical abilities.

My concrete commitment is to treat accessibility as part of the scholarship, not something added after the site is completed. I will use clear headings, readable fonts, strong color contrast, descriptive links, alternative text for images, and transcripts for audio or video content. I will avoid unnecessary jargon and explain specialized theological language when it is needed. I will also test my pages on a mobile device and invite someone else to navigate the site before I publish it.

If digitization is human labor, then accessibility expresses care for the humans who encounter that labor. My goal is not merely to place my work online. It is to create a digital table with enough room and enough access for the academy, the church, and the community to sit together.
