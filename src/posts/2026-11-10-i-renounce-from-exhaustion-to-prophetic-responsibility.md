## Sleep cannot resolve this weariness.

It comes from repeatedly explaining why your community deserves investment, why your history deserves preservation, and why your children deserve opportunities others take for granted. It grows when institutions celebrate your resilience while leaving the conditions that require it untouched.

This exhaustion reaches into the spirit. We can continue showing up, fulfilling responsibilities, and speaking the expected words while wondering how much more of ourselves the struggle will demand. Against that exhaustion, two words can become a declaration of moral agency: I renounce.

In reflecting on Dr. Jamal Harrison Bryant’s sermon, I hear an invitation to consider what those words can make possible. My reading is that renunciation can move us toward responsibility: naming what we can no longer accommodate so that we can recover the capacity to serve.

As a pastor, public theologian, and proud member of Omega Psi Phi Fraternity, Incorporated, I find that possibility compelling. It asks something of my faith and my fraternity membership. It asks what kind of person my commitments are forming, and whose life becomes more livable because I honor them.

## Hearing the Pain Before Defending the Institution

The debate over denouncing or renouncing Divine Nine membership requires careful listening. To denounce generally means to condemn publicly. Renouncing means relinquishing a claim, commitment, or allegiance. A person may do both. Yet explaining the distinction cannot substitute for hearing the experiences behind either decision. Some people leave institutions carrying wounds. They may describe humiliation, coercion, exclusion, spiritual conflict, or the disappointment of discovering that an organization’s conduct contradicts its stated ideals. We owe those accounts attention. My love for Omega cannot become a reason to dismiss someone else’s pain. Another person’s departure should not determine the meaning of my membership. The moral question is whether we're willing to examine what actually happens within the institutions we defend. Who can speak? Who receives protection? Whose knowledge counts? What happens when a person with little institutional power tells an uncomfortable truth? These questions belong in fraternity houses, churches, and universities. Institutional loyalty becomes dangerous when it teaches us to distrust the wounded before examining the wound.

## The Brotherhood That Helped Me See It Through

A history of grief shapes how I understand brotherhood. I lost my father during my first year in college, just before taking my first exam. I had no biological brothers to help me navigate the painful waters of that loss. Years later, as a grown man, I found Omega Psi Phi a brotherhood that became part of the community sustaining my life. That belonging mattered because I had spent so much of my life hearing that I was not smart enough, strong enough, or good enough. Over time, those judgments became hard to separate from my own sense of self. Du Bois gives me language for examining that struggle. In *The Souls of Black Folk*, he describes double consciousness as the experience of “always looking at one’s self through the eyes of others.” His argument concerns Black life within a society structured by racial exclusion. I draw upon that insight to understand how judgments about Black capacity can enter the inner life and become measures against which we assess our own possibilities. I recognize in his account the painful tension between the person I believed I could become and the person other people’s judgments permitted me to imagine.[^2]

My family and fraternity brothers helped me question those judgments. Their encouragement supported my decision to pursue scholarship at the highest academic level. Through them, I experienced uplift: people took my intellectual aspirations seriously and encouraged me to do the same.

Then came another dark season. After completing the coursework in a previous doctoral program, I was told I would not be able to finish. That disappointment reopened an old wound. Once again, I heard the familiar verdict: You are not good enough. My family, a few close friends, and my fraternity brothers stood with me. They reminded me of Edgar A. Guest’s poem:

> **“See It Through” — Edgar A. Guest**
>
> Black may be the clouds about you,  
> And your future may seem grim,  
> But don’t let your nerve desert you;  
> Keep yourself in fighting trim.[^3]

Those words gained force as people reminded me of them. They knew my disappointment and kept affirming my capacity. Their encouragement helped me understand perseverance as something a community can nurture when a person struggles to sustain it alone. This is one way Omega has contributed to my moral formation. Receiving that kind of care creates a sense of obligation. Having needed encouragement, I must consider whose aspirations need my support. Having experienced the weight of another person’s judgment, I must be careful about the judgments I place upon others. One of my closest fraternity brothers, Reggie Duckett, continues to teach me that lesson. While battling illness, he continues to serve God, serve our community, and help provide scholarships for young men heading to college. His example makes uplift concrete. Even amid his own uncertainty, he remains concerned about somebody else’s future. I honor his service and the person who offers it. His worth does not depend upon how much he continued.

These experiences shape how I hear the declaration, “I Renounce.”

*I renounce the verdict that another person’s assessment exhausts the truth of who I am.*  
*I renounce the belief that would turn an academic setback into a judgment of my worth.*  
*I renounce a model of manhood that leaves men unable to acknowledge grief or receive help.*

And I accept the responsibility that follows: to encourage, accompany, serve, and make room for another person’s flourishing. My experience cannot settle every question about fraternity life, nor erase the pain of those whose experiences differ from mine. It does explain why I approach this conversation with gratitude and accountability. For me, brotherhood has helped transform exhaustion into the capacity to continue. That is where renunciation becomes a resource for perseverance: I can refuse judgments that diminish my humanity while recommitting to the relationships and responsibilities that sustain it.

## Exhaustion and the Inner Life

Howard Washington Thurman’s *Jesus and the Disinherited* examines the pressures that oppression places upon the inner life, including fear, deception, and hatred.[^4] His work helps me ask what prolonged struggle does to our ability to remain truthful, loving, and whole. I bring that concern to the exhausted person who says, “I renounce.” Sometimes the declaration names a necessary limit; I can no longer participate in practices that require my silence. I can no longer accept humiliation as the price of belonging. I can no longer give every available part of myself to a system that refuses accountability.

Perseverance needs discernment about what deserves our continued effort. It also needs rest, companionship, and the freedom to change how we participate. We must not turn perseverance into another demand that wounded people absorb unlimited injury.

I understand renunciation here as releasing an accommodation that has been consuming the strength needed for faithful action. I renounce the expectation that enduring injustice quietly proves my spiritual maturity.

That declaration creates room for endurance sustained by dignity, solidarity, and purpose.

## William James and the Difference a Declaration Makes

William James offers a method for sharpening this argument. In the second lecture of *Pragmatism*, he examines the meaning of ideas through the practical differences they make in experience.[^5] Applied to this sermon, his method directs attention to what follows the words. What changes when a congregation says, “I renounce”? Does a declaration alter its budget, leadership practices, community relationships, or how it treats people who dissent? Does it generate courage to challenge injustice? Does it help exhausted people recover the ability to act together? These are my applications of James’s method. They do not mean that whatever benefits an institution becomes morally true. They require us to identify consequences and examine them. My own experience gives these questions substance. Encouragement helped me continue pursuing scholarship. My brother’s commitment helps young men move toward college. The principles of our fraternity acquire meaning through such relationships and practices.[^6]

If renunciation leaves our practices untouched, its public significance remains limited. If it protects those harmed, sustains community work, and demands accountable leadership, we can begin to see its meaning enacted. The declaration must acquire a life beyond the microphone.

## Cornel West and the Recovery of Prophetic Voice

Cornel West’s prophetic pragmatism brings cultural criticism into relationship with political engagement. I draw on that relationship to ask how moral judgment becomes responsible public action.[^7] In his 2017 Harvard Divinity School convocation address, West warns against becoming “well-adjusted to injustice.” He connects prophetic resistance with character formation, attention to suffering, and a willingness to bear costs.[^8]

That warning troubles me as a pastor and an educator. We can teach people how to succeed within institutions while leaving them unable, or unwilling, to question the suffering those institutions produce. Reclaiming prophetic voice requires learning whose testimony we have ignored, examining how power protects itself, and accepting that truthful speech may threaten our own comfort. It requires accounting for whose knowledge is treated as authoritative and whose experience is dismissed before it is heard. This examination must include Christian nationalism wherever Christian identity becomes a justification for national superiority, or unquestioning loyalty to political power. My theological objection is that such allegiance can train believers to protect dominance while overlooking the neighbor. The same examination reaches inward. Our churches and fraternities must ask where prestige has displaced service and where access to power has weakened our willingness to criticize it. A prophetic voice needs the freedom to question its own institution.

## Faith Beyond the Size of the Crowd

Judges 7 adds another dimension. In Gideon's account, God reduces the army so Israel cannot claim deliverance as an achievement of its own strength.[^9] I read that passage as a challenge to our confidence in numbers, visibility, and institutional influence. It does not establish that people who leave contemporary organizations are faithless, or that those who remain are God’s chosen few. It asks a more searching question: What have we mistaken for the source of our strength? A crowded sanctuary can still lack moral courage. A distinguished membership roster can still leave suffering unattended. Faithfulness requires us to examine how we use our collective strength. Du Bois’s account of the Black church as a center of social and organizational life underscores the public stakes of that examination. Institutions gather people and resources; their moral significance includes what those resources make possible.[^10] Our task is to make belonging answerable to human flourishing.

## A Declaration That Reaches Beyond Us

Near the beginning of his “Letter from Birmingham Jail,” Martin Luther King Jr. writes:

> Injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere.[^11]

That claim prevents this reflection from ending with the defense of my own organization. It extends my responsibility beyond people who share my letters, theology, race, or institutional home. The pain of someone who leaves must matter to me. So must the flourishing of someone who remains. Our public witness must reach people who have never belonged to either our chapters or our churches.

Here, “I renounce” becomes a declaration through which we reclaim our prophetic voice.

*I renounce the silence that protects harm.*  
*I renounce the prestige that makes suffering easy to overlook.*  
*I renounce the demand that exhausted people prove their faith by accepting another injury.*
*And I commit myself to the work these refusals require: listening, repair, accountability, and sustained service.*

*I remain a proud Omega man. That pride places obligations upon me. My Christian faith deepens the examination of how I fulfill them.*

When the declaration ends, a neighbor will still need an advocate.  
A young person will still need someone dependable.  
A person harmed by an institution will still need truth and protection.

Let our renunciation recover the courage and capacity to answer them.
