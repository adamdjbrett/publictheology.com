Blog Post 4

As a campus pastor, I have seen young adults come to college, interested in various humanities, but told, by either parents, high school teachers, or even narratives within society itself, that studying humanities subjects is a path to loads of student debt and no career prospects. One young man graduated with a degree in both microbiology and religious studies, spent a year after graduation working in a lab, only to find the atmosphere toxic. He is now a PhD student at Princeton University researching American religious history as it intersects with medicine. The sciences are no guarantee of a good job.

Nonetheless, the narratives continue. So much so, in fact, that bolstering real concrete engagement has become a significant mission in the humanities world. The University of Michigan History program has sought to create history labs. James W. Cook and Rita Chin outline this work in a case study, “History Labs Building a More Effective Case for the Power and Efficacy of Humanistic Training.”[^1] They have partnered with various faculty members to create collaborative projects around a number of topics: policing, immigration, environmental studies, medieval London, Holocaust research. This real world engagement opens the understanding of history beyond the solo researcher producing obscure tomes for a small audience of experts.

The challenge, of course, is that this model is new. This way of thinking about how to teach history and research methods is a culture shift away from centuries-long entrenched mode of scholarship. Spreadsheets of data, new visualizations, podcasts, interactive maps, and more expand the understanding of what the subject “History” is about. Take, [The Fallen of World War II](https://www.neilfilms.com/ww2/), for example. This project provides an interactive animation that shows the devastating loss of life in World War II. The data is presented in ways that move beyond tables of numbers. At some point, most human beings lose sense of the scale of numbers. A million is too large for most humans to truly understand. The Fallen helps provide scale in its animations and visualizations. By including the fatalities of non-combatants, new insight of the deadly nature of that war is revealed.

Interestingly, I too moved from scientific research to theology. Leaning on a liberal arts education, I shifted gears and faced my own cultural shift. There was a shift from a PhD program studying acoustics and low temperature physics, but more importantly there is shift within theological research for me.

In my first year of seminary, I took a class “Film and Theology” with Dr. Phil Baker. Analyzing popular movies for theological content was not the model of doing theology I had in my head, that is, just sitting in the library consulting Greek and Hebrew dictionaries writing papers on my own. Dr. Baker gave us permission to turn our theological gaze toward the cultural, and popular culture even, arena. Now, I find myself engaging comic books and graphic novels as a viable avenue for theological analysis. [Graphic Religion](https://graphicreligion.omeka.net/) is the beginning of my start to analyze religious content, narrative and image, in comic books and graphic novels. Because of the general assumption that comic books are the territory of superheroes (for sure, they are), some might believe that nothing else dwells there (for sure, much else does).  Within the stories of superheroes, narratives wrestle with what it means to be human, and thus, religious narrative emerges. Beyond superheroes, many genres subsist in comic books and graphic novels. They provide a subculture in which a theological imagination intersects with sequential art, telling stories that seek meaning in our shared human existence.

I have been engaged in one sort or another of public digital theology across most of my ordained ministry, blogging, podcasts, and now this. I continue to learn and grow. Early on in my ministry, I wrote in a way that remained consistent with academic theology. I wrote as an expert (hardly) delivering my knowledge to the masses. That perspective shifts in my podcasting. Co-hosts and guests became the way to interrogate material together. At times, I was the facilitator of a conversation among folks from whom I learned.

Now, I see the shift even more. The work of public theology is inherently collaborative. My work is shaped by the conversations I have with the staff at my friendly, local, comic store (Shoutout to Phantom of the Attic comics!). My research is far beyond what I thought it would be. I am not dispensing learning from a privileged position.  I am in regular conversation with folks from all sorts of contexts.

Public theology is a work that begins in the midst of everyday life. It is a project that takes the human condition seriously, is committed to discussing the positives and negatives, seeks to ensure the positives are available to all, and the negatives are reformed so all might prosper.
