In the last post I argued, following Namsoon Kang, that public theology needs a cosmopolitan grammar. Here I take the argument into the classroom. I will argue that a cosmopolitan public theology requires a cosmopolitan pedagogy, and that such a pedagogy proceeds by dis-location: it unsettles the vertical classroom, it makes the professor a theologian without a passport, and it treats student knowledge as a public rather than an audience. I make the case with Kang again at the center, with Kwame Anthony Appiah and Jacques Derrida alongside, and with Paulo Freire and Denise Taliaferro Baszile as the pedagogical interlocutors they need.

## The vertical classroom

The classroom has traditionally been imagined as a vertically integrated space. Knowledge is made elsewhere (the archive, the laboratory, the seminar table) and carried down to students, who receive it. Freire named the model half a century ago: the professor as banker, depositing content into accounts that passively accept it (*Pedagogy of the Oppressed*, 1970). Elisabeth Schüssler Fiorenza's term for the structure the banking model serves is kyriarchy, the interlocking rule of lord over servant, male over female, colonizer over colonized, one that no single axis of identity exhausts. The vertical classroom is kyriarchal not because any individual professor is a tyrant but because the architecture assumes that knowledge flows one way and that the person at the lectern is its source.

A public theology that criticizes domination in the square while reproducing it in the seminar room has a credibility problem. Kang's fourth commitment, counter-empire, asks us to scrutinize "our own abuses of power," not only those of others. The classroom is where theologians hold the most power with the least scrutiny.

## Dis-location, or being out of place

Kang's cosmopolitan theology moves "across boundaries without diminishing the relevance and value of particular identities." Translated into pedagogy, I call this dis-location: a deliberate practice of being out of place. Three sources converge here. Transnational feminism supplies the insight that identities are hybrid and liminal, lived along axes (gender, race, class, sexuality, nation, religion) that rarely line up. Queer theory supplies the suspicion that "the normal" in any institution is an achievement of power rather than a fact of nature, and that knowledge production itself can be queered. Kang supplies the theological warrant: the one who is "from everywhere and from nowhere" is not rootless but trans-identified, holding multiple solidarities at once.

Appiah keeps dis-location honest. His "partial cosmopolitan" refuses both the view from nowhere and the retreat into the local; his formula is universality plus difference. A dis-located classroom therefore does not pretend the professor has no location. It names the location (my own: a white, Christian, settler scholar teaching on Haudenosaunee and other Indigenous lands) and then declines to let that location be the final word. Appiah's further observation, that people live together long before they agree about why, is a pedagogical principle in disguise. Students need not share first principles to share a practice of inquiry. The conversation is the course.

## The professor without a passport

Kang's figure of the "theologian without a passport" becomes, in the classroom, the professor without a passport. A passport is the document that conditions welcome: it says who may enter, for how long, and on what terms. The syllabus is a passport. So is the rubric, the canon, the register of acceptable prose, and the unspoken knowledge of which questions are "really" theological. Derrida's analysis of hospitality (*Of Hospitality*, 2000; *On Cosmopolitanism and Forgiveness*, 2001) names the aporia precisely: unconditional hospitality is impossible as policy, because every institution has conditions, yet the conditional welcome of the actual classroom is only intelligible against the unconditional demand it betrays.

The professor without a passport works within that aporia rather than resolving it. In practice this means several things. The professor dissociates, as Kang puts it, from any discourse that files a student into a stereotypical box (the "diversity" student, the "conservative" student, the student who is "not a strong writer"). The professor treats the arrivant, the student whose question does not fit, as the one who may reorganize the course rather than the one who has misunderstood it. The professor keeps conditions (there are deadlines; there is a canon to be read before it is argued with) but holds them as conditions, revisable and owned, not as nature.

## Student knowledge as a public

Public theology, in Tracy's formulation, addresses three publics: the academy, the church, and society. A cosmopolitan pedagogy adds that the classroom is itself a public and that students are not its audience but its members. Kang's trans-religious solidarity rests on "an ethic of transformation" rather than an ethic of balance; dialogue should revisit and reconstruct the foundations of each participant's discourse. The classroom version is a set of transgressive practices that revalue who gets to make knowledge: counterstories (Baszile's term for narratives that talk back to the majoritarian account), captured notes that circulate student thinking as course text, multiple modes of speaking (the paper, the thread, the podcast segment, the sermon), and the integration of praxis with theory so that the field placement and the seminar are one conversation rather than two.

None of these practices is novel on its own. What makes them cosmopolitan public theology is the end they serve. Kang's fifth commitment, boundary-transcending solidarity for "all living beings," means the classroom is not training students to join a guild; it is forming theologians who can cross borders in order to practice neighborly love and to stand with those on the margins. Counterstories are how the margins enter the room.

## What this asks of us

Following Kang, and extending her with Appiah and Derrida, I argue that a cosmopolitan public theological pedagogy asks three things. First, name the location and then dis-locate it: the professor speaks from somewhere and refuses to make that somewhere the measure of all knowledge. Second, hold the conditions of the classroom as conditions: keep the passport in a drawer, know it is there, and be willing to let the arrivant rewrite it. Third, treat student knowledge as public theology in its own right, which means the counterstory is primary source, not anecdote.

Whose land does your classroom sit on? Which identities does your syllabus privilege and which does it exclude? A public theology that cannot answer those questions at home has no business asking them in the square.
