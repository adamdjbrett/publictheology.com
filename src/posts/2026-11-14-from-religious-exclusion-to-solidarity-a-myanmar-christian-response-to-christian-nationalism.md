As a Christian from Myanmar living in the United States, I am concerned when people who have experienced religious exclusion become attracted to religious nationalism in their new country. In my own observations, some Myanmar Christians welcome the idea of a Christian America after experiencing the pressures of Buddhist dominance and Burmanization. I do not suggest that all Myanmar Christians share this position or that their experiences explain every political choice. Rather, I want to examine a troubling possibility: suffering under one form of religious dominance does not automatically prevent us from supporting another. For someone accustomed to insecurity as a religious minority, a nation that privileges Christianity may appear to promise protection and belonging. Yet that promise requires careful theological examination. Christian nationalism merges Christian and American identities and seeks privileges that can diminish the equal belonging of others (Christians Against Christian Nationalism, n.d.). I argue that Christian obedience should lead us to challenge religious superiority, including within our own communities. Our experiences of exclusion should become a foundation for solidarity with others.

Understanding this concern requires distinguishing religious dominance from Burmanization. Burmanization involves promoting Bamar language, culture, and identity as standards of national belonging. Buddhist dominance concerns privileging Buddhism in ways that marginalize other religious communities. These processes overlap, but ethnicity and religion cannot simply be treated as identical. A United Nations report describes how military-led nation-building promoted Burman culture, the Burmese language, and Buddhism while restricting minority identities and religious practices (United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights, 2020, paras. 10–11). Criticizing these practices does not justify treating all Buddhists or Bamar people as supporters of oppression. Likewise, examining Christian nationalism requires attention to particular ideas and practices rather than condemning Christians collectively. Myanmar and the United States have different histories, institutions, and relationships between religion and power. My comparison concerns a shared danger: making one religious identity a measure of national belonging. I suggest that some migrants’ attraction to Christian nationalism may reflect a desire for security after exclusion. Understanding that desire, however, does not establish that religious privilege provides a just response to it.

Christian Scripture offers resources for examining this desire for protection through political power. Romans 13:1–7 calls for submission to governing authorities while describing their responsibility to serve the good and restrain wrongdoing (NRSVUE, 2021, Romans 13:1–7). I read this passage as requiring attention to the purposes of authority, rather than granting rulers unlimited approval. Acts records the apostles giving priority to obedience to God when authorities command them to stop teaching in Jesus’ name (NRSVUE, 2021, Acts 5:29). These passages address different situations, but together they challenge unconditional political submission. Jesus also teaches his disciples to practice leadership through service rather than domination (NRSVUE, 2021, Mark 10:42–45). This teaching shapes my understanding of God’s sovereignty as a just and compassionate reign. No government becomes a faithful expression of that reign simply by adopting Christian language. Political authority must be evaluated through its service to neighbors and its treatment of vulnerable lives. Barth expresses the limit clearly: “We cannot obey man in disobedience to God” (Barth, 2012, p. 310).

This understanding calls Christians to examine both political authority and our own desires for superiority. Being a religious minority does not make a community free from exclusionary attitudes. We can suffer discrimination while still believing that people outside our faith deserve less influence or recognition. When such attitudes remain unexamined, migration may change our position without changing our understanding of power. Theological self-examination therefore involves asking whether the freedom we seek for ourselves also extends to Muslims, Buddhists, Jews, Indigenous practitioners, and people without religious affiliation. Repentance requires acknowledging our participation in injustice while continuing practical responsibility toward others (H. R. Niebuhr, 2012, p. 258). This should lead to accountable action. Christians can challenge policies and rhetoric that make belonging depend on religious identity, while examining the fairness of their own methods. Resistance should protect life, include affected communities, welcome correction, and refuse dehumanization. Christian conviction can motivate public participation without requiring that the state give Christians greater standing than their neighbors. The credibility of our witness depends partly on whether we defend others’ dignity when doing so brings no advantage to our own community.

Solidarity gives this responsibility practical form. Remembering oppressed people’s experiences can challenge dominant histories and help restore their agency (Metz, 1980, pp. 66–67). For Myanmar Christian communities in the United States, this could mean connecting memories of exclusion with a commitment to religious freedom for everyone. Churches can examine preaching about national identity, invite neighbors of different faiths to share their experiences, support families facing discrimination, and discuss how religious nationalism affects public belonging. Such encounters should allow neighbors to shape the conversation rather than serve merely as examples for Christian reflection. We should also listen when others describe Christianity as a source of exclusion, even when their testimony unsettles our self-understanding. Holding Christian convictions firmly remains compatible with recognizing that others deserve equal dignity and a meaningful voice in common life. My hope is that Myanmar Christians’ experiences will help us recognize the dangers of religious superiority wherever it appears. Christian obedience becomes public witness when we challenge domination, examine our own use of power, and accompany vulnerable neighbors. The freedom we seek becomes more credible when we defend it for others.
