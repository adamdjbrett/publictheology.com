When you look at a map of your hometown, what do you see? Roads, rivers, and familiar names may appear. But whose stories are missing? A map rarely tells us everything about the people who have lived there, cared for the land, or been forced to leave. Similar questions arise when we build digital archives—online collections of photographs, documents, recordings, and other historical materials. Choosing what to preserve shapes how people understand the past. These decisions also affect communities whose histories have been ignored or misrepresented. Preserving their stories carries a responsibility to consider how these communities understand their own experiences.

Rico Devara Chapman explores this responsibility in “Building and Sustaining Africana Digital Humanities at HBCUs.” He argues that digital scholarship should center Black voices and recover community knowledge through collaboration. Historically Black colleges and universities have an important role because of their relationships with surrounding communities. Chapman also introduces an Indigenous understanding of technology that includes storytelling, ancestral communication, and spirit. This understanding connects technology with human needs for community, harmony, and meaning. Africana digital humanities therefore goes beyond collecting information and sharing it online. Recovering stories can help communities harmed by racism and displacement reconnect with their histories and affirm their dignity. An archive can contribute to healing when community members participate in preserving and interpreting their experiences. Such healing grows through relationships and shared responsibility, rather than simply through putting materials online (Debates in the Digital Humanities 2023, pp. 240–242).

This perspective helps me evaluate Native Land Digital, an Indigenous-led project mapping Indigenous territories, languages, and treaties. Although primarily a mapping resource, it raises questions that also matter for archives: whose knowledge guides the collection, and how does its presentation shape understanding? By centering Indigenous relationships with land, it encourages visitors to reconsider familiar national borders. Its searchable design allows visitors to explore a location and encounter names and histories they may not know, while sources and links encourage further learning. However, I see a limitation: mapped areas can make complex relationships appear fixed or certain. A map cannot fully convey a community’s internal differences, shifting experiences, or spiritual relationships with the land. Native Land Digital acknowledges that its map does not establish official or legal boundaries and encourages visitors to learn directly from Indigenous nations. I understand this as an invitation to continue listening.

These questions matter as I begin developing Christianity in Myanmar: Places, People, and Histories. As I consider describing and arranging source materials, I am surprised by how much interpretation enters seemingly simple decisions. Organizing a timeline around missionaries’ arrival dates, for example, could make local Christian leadership less visible. Short descriptions might overlook women’s contributions or differences among ethnic communities. My work in public theology—reflecting on how Christian faith shapes our shared responsibilities—calls me to approach these choices with care. Communities carry memories of suffering, survival, and hope. My responsibility includes acknowledging uncertainty, seeking permission when needed, protecting sensitive information, and welcoming corrections. Before sharing a story, I want to ask: Who participated in creating and presenting this story? Whose voice is missing? And how might preserving this memory serve the community to whom it belongs?
