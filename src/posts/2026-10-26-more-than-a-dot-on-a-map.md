In ministry, a connection can mean the difference between someone suffering alone and someone receiving a timely visit, a meal, or a word of encouragement. That is why network analysis interests me. It offers a way to examine relationships while raising a deeper question. What responsibilities come with making those relationships visible?

Network analysis is a digital humanities method that studies how people, organizations, or other entities connect. These entities are called “nodes,” and their connections are called “edges.” A congregation’s ministries might be nodes, while referrals, communication, and shared service form the edges. The method can help us ask who connects different groups, where support concentrates, and who might be overlooked.

For my network sketch, I propose a congregational care network linking pastoral leadership, the Pastor of Congregational Care and Ministry Services, deacons, bereavement volunteers, greeters, and households. Pastoral leadership connects with the care pastor and deacons; these groups coordinate support with bereavement volunteers. Greeters connect households to the care ministry, while households also connect directly with deacons. These overlapping relationships suggest that care should travel through several pathways. The sketch represents roles rather than naming individuals or revealing private needs.

Stanford’s [Mapping the Republic of Letters](http://republicofletters.stanford.edu/) provides a historical example of studying connections. The project uses visualizations to investigate networks of early modern scholarly exchange. Its strength is helping readers see patterns that would be difficult to recognize through individual letters alone. Scholarship emerges as something sustained through relationships across places and time.

My critique concerns what such a map can leave invisible. Surviving correspondence does not represent every relationship that mattered. A prominent node may reflect extensive archival preservation as well as influence. People whose letters were lost, whose knowledge circulated orally, or whose work supported celebrated scholars may receive less attention. Absence from a visualization cannot establish absence from history.

Brown, Whaanga, and Lewis emphasize relationality, reciprocity, accountability, and Indigenous communities’ authority over their data. Although their chapter addresses artificial intelligence, I take its principles as a challenge to network research: communities should help determine how their relationships and knowledge are represented. Indigenous protocols arise from specific communities and deserve more than being reduced to a generic checklist.

Platform choices also carry ethical consequences. In “Dear Scholars, Delete Your Account at Academia.edu,” Sarah Bond argues that the platform’s commercial interests, monetization of scholarship, and status-oriented paid analytics conflict with open scholarly exchange. She identifies nonprofit alternatives, including [Humanities Commons](https://hcommons.org/). For me, the contrast concerns whether a network serves shared learning or turns scholarly participation into a commercial asset.

Yet openness still requires care; Boyles, Petersen, and Jacob explain that even open-source projects can depend on technologies that enable surveillance. Applied to ministry, this means a public care map could unintentionally expose someone’s vulnerability.

As a pastor and public theologian, I must seek consent, protect confidentiality, acknowledge overlooked labor, and let participants correct representations of their lives. A network sketch can help identify gaps in care, but it cannot measure anyone’s worth. Behind every node is a neighbor. My responsibility is to ensure that making connections visible helps people receive care with dignity.

## Proposed congregational care network

A sketch of communication, referrals, and coordinated support.

- **Pastoral Leadership** — Pastor of Congregational Care, Deacons
- **Pastor of Congregational Care** — Deacons, Bereavement Volunteers, Greeters
- **Deacons** — Bereavement Volunteers, Households
- **Greeters** — Households

Reading the sketch: each role is a node; each connection is an edge. Connections represent communication and support, not authority or a person’s value. This is a proposed role-based sketch, not a record of private care needs.
