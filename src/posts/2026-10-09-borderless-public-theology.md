In November 2013 I sat in a convention center in Busan, South Korea, at the Tenth Assembly of the World Council of Churches (WCC), praying for the "visible unity" of the church while my own country built a wall along its southern border and kept Indigenous nations on reservations. Which side was I on? To whom did I pledge allegiance? Which passport did I travel under? Those questions became a paper for the Global Ecumenical Theological Institute, "Theologians Without Borders," and they are the questions I want to bring to public theology here. I will argue that a public theology worth the name must be borderless: it must refuse the nation-state as its organizing frame, start from difference rather than unity, and treat the border itself as a theological problem. I make the case through my experience in Busan, Namsoon Kang's "ecumenical taboo," and Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak's "new culturalist alibi."

## The nation-state at the ecumenical table

The WCC describes itself as a space for "breaking down barriers between people." In Busan I watched barriers reassert themselves in the gentlest possible way. The host committee's cultural plenary told the history of Korea through the violence it suffered under China and Japan; those were the only mentions of China or Japan in any plenary, though Chinese and Japanese churches sat in the hall. The China Christian Council's response ran in the assembly newspaper in simplified Chinese only. The Korean prime minister delivered the customary head-of-state welcome. None of this was malicious, and I do not single out Korea; the pattern repeats wherever Christians gather internationally. The point is structural. The first question at any such gathering is "where are you from?" (often followed by "where are you from originally?" when the answer does not match the questioner's racial expectations), and the answer files a person under a flag before it files them under a confession or a cosmovision.

Christianity's great gift, as I understood it then and understand it now, is its capacity to bring people together across the boundaries of the nation-state. Yet the Peace Train that was meant to carry the assembly's hope for Korean reunification could not cross the thirty-eighth parallel, and the Christ at the Checkpoint conference my GETI colleague Munther Isaac helped organize takes its name from a wall. Where is the prophetic witness of the church when it succumbs to the nation-state? Public theology inherits that question, because public theology addresses "society," and in practice society is almost always imagined as a national society. A public theology that takes the nation as its public has already conceded the border.

## Unity conceals difference

The language of "visible unity" has a second cost. Unity tends to be found by locating a truth claim everyone can affirm, then distilling it until it is inert. Peace, justice, and reconciliation become, as Derrida warned, signifiers without referents; each party signs the document and assigns the words different content. The Lima text on Baptism, Eucharist, and Ministry brought real convergence and still names the differences that remain. Unity imposed from above is imperial and yields no true consensus, and it does not work at all for interreligious dialogue, where no shared theological concept is available to unify around.

Following Luce Irigaray, I proposed in 2014 that ecumenical work start from difference, and I make the same proposal now for public theology. Difference affirms the singular plurality of persons; it treats the ways sex, gender, race, class, and place shape our thought as a frame for thinking through problems rather than a problem to be overcome. Consensus built from difference is slow. It is also the only consensus that holds, because people's theologies and practices are the starting point of the work rather than what must be bracketed to reach a universal.

## The ecumenical taboo and the culturalist alibi

Kang names the mechanism by which unity does its concealing. The "ecumenical taboo" is the unwritten rule that topics may be engaged only at their most banal level and that critique and disagreement must be tabled to preserve the idea of unity. Tolerance has achieved real goods; Kang does not deny it and neither do I. But the taboo keeps the legacies of injustice off the agenda, and public theology, which exists to put injustice on the agenda, cannot operate under it. Kang's remedy is a shift from "as-a-discourse" (speaking as a woman, as a Korean, as a Baptist, in an ever longer list of markers) to "with-discourse," a politics of solidarity that is rooted in race and class and attentive to how they mingle with sex, sexuality, and the rest.

Spivak supplies the warning that keeps difference honest. The "new culturalist alibi" insists on the continuity of a native tradition untouched by the West and thereby "legitimizes the very thing it claims to combat." Difference fetishized becomes an alibi: a reason to refuse engagement, a license to let sexism or homophobia pass as culture. The alibi also preserves a paternalism in which the West plays progressive while "the rest" plays authentic. Edward Said made the same point from another direction; no one is just one label, and to reduce a person to one is to reproduce the logic of empire. Borderless public theology therefore walks a narrow path. It affirms difference against unity and refuses the alibi that difference can become.

## Theology without borders, theologians without passports

Chandra Talpade Mohanty writes of a "feminism without borders" that attends to "the simultaneous plurality and narrowness of borders and the emancipatory potential of crossing through, with, and over these borders in our everyday lives." I want a public theology without borders in the same sense. Borders divide and wound; they are also, as Mohanty insists, real, and the task is to think through them rather than wish them away. The partition of the Indian subcontinent, the thirty-eighth parallel, the wall at the checkpoint, the lines that divide Indigenous nations across what is now the US–Canada border. Each makes the invisible visible on the land and on bodies; each is a theological datum as well as a political one.

The theologian who does this work travels without a passport, in Kang's figure, which is to say the theologian names their location and declines to let it be the last word. Mine is specific: a Baptist formed by the Wesleyan-Holiness and peace church traditions, a US citizen, a scholar on Haudenosaunee land. I cannot shed any of that, and I should not try. What I can do is refuse to let the flag organize the conversation. Sara Ahmed observes that we only know which way to turn once we know which way we are facing. Borderless public theology faces the other, body to body, across whatever line has been drawn between us, and asks what the two of us can do from there. Perhaps the most important part of public theology, as of ecumenical theology, is disorientation.
