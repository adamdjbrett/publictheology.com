Whose land do you live on, and whose neighbor are you? I will argue that public theology, if it is to be inclusive and interreligious rather than merely tolerant, needs a cosmopolitan grammar, and that Namsoon Kang supplies the best one we have. I will make the case by reading Kang's "Toward a Cosmopolitan Theology: Constructing Public Theology from the Future" (in *Planetary Loves*, 2011) through two fellow travelers she names, Kwame Anthony Appiah and Jacques Derrida, and by showing where each adds something to her account.

## Kang: public theology from the future

Kang's argument is that cosmopolitan theology is "a new form of public theology." The phrase matters. She does not offer cosmopolitanism as a branch of ethics or a mood of world citizenship; she offers it as a way of doing theology in public, and she grounds it in a double diagnosis. On one side, our context of "cross-cultural interaction and multiple-layered identities" calls for a theology that moves across boundaries without diminishing the particular identities people actually live in. On the other, we live amid "unprecedented inequality between nations, regions and people," which calls for a theology that crosses borders in order to practice "impartial neighborly love" and to stand with those on the margins. Cosmopolitan theology holds both: it is boundary crossing and it is partisan for the oppressed.

From that diagnosis Kang develops five commitments. 1) Trans-identity: a move from the politics of single identity to "multiple solidarities across various identities," without abandoning the attachments one holds dear. 2) Radical affirmation of the other, which she glosses as radical neighborly love for the racialized, gendered, sexualized, religious, and ethnic other. 3) Trans-religious solidarity, grounded in an ethic of transformation rather than an ethic of balance. 4) Counter-empire, a scrutiny of hegemony that includes "our own abuses of power," not only the abuses of others. 5) Boundary-transcending solidarity, a planetary solidarity for "all living beings."

The third commitment is where Kang earns her keep for public theology. Traditional interreligious dialogue, she argues, has rested on "an ethics of tolerance." Tolerance eases antagonism between religions, which is a real good (I do not want to be glib about it). It also leaves untouched "the very fundamental issues of injustice that each of our religions has practiced and perpetuated," and it typically neglects gender justice, sexuality, the theological construction of the other, violence, and the cooption of religion by empire. Kang wants dialogue to become a process of "re-visiting, re-challenging, and re-constructing the very foundations of each religious discourse" from the standpoint of justice as it lands on ordinary lives. The theologian who does this work is, in her memorable figure, a "theologian without a passport." Such a theologian names their identities as present without treating them as the final word on who they are, and "intentionally dissociates" from any discourse that files a person into a stereotypical box.

## Appiah: universality plus difference

Kwame Anthony Appiah's *Cosmopolitanism: Ethics in a World of Strangers* (2006) gives Kang's first commitment a philosophical spine. Appiah's formula is "universality plus difference": we owe obligations to every human being, and we take seriously the particular lives, practices, and beliefs that give those human beings their meaning. He calls the cosmopolitan a "partial cosmopolitan," one who refuses the choice between rootless universalism and defensive localism. Kang's trans-identity is this partiality rendered theologically; her refusal to make people abandon their attachments is Appiah's refusal to make the cosmopolitan homeless.

Appiah adds one thing Kang's account needs. He insists that cosmopolitan conversation does not require agreement on first principles. People live together, he observes, long before they agree about why; we can share practices and judgments while our justifications differ. For an interreligious public theology this observation is liberating. The Haudenosaunee, the Lutheran, and the Muslim need not resolve their cosmovisions before working together against a pipeline or a deportation. The conversation (Appiah's preferred word) is the practice, not a preliminary to it. Where Appiah falls short of Kang is in the fourth commitment: his cosmopolitanism is light on empire. Kang's counter-empire supplies what Appiah's genial universalism leaves out, namely that the strangers in his title are often strangers because someone's border made them so.

## Derrida: hospitality and the aporia

Derrida takes the border itself as his object. In *On Cosmopolitanism and Forgiveness* (2001) he recovers the medieval "cities of refuge" and asks what an unconditional hospitality would look like: a welcome offered to the arrivant before any question of name, papers, or passport. He knows the answer. Unconditional hospitality is impossible as policy, because every actual city has laws, and laws condition. Yet the conditional hospitality of visas and quotas is only intelligible against the unconditional demand it betrays. Hospitality is aporetic; one must act within the aporia rather than resolve it.

Kang's "theologian without a passport" is a Derridean figure whether or not she flags it. The passport is the conditioning document par excellence; to set it aside, even provisionally, is to practice the unconditional within the conditional. Derrida also sharpens Kang's second commitment. Radical affirmation of the other is not a warm feeling; it is a structural impossibility that must be attempted anyway, and the attempt is where ethics lives. In this way Derrida keeps cosmopolitan theology from becoming a program. It remains a practice of the impossible, which is what a theology of neighborly love has always been.

## What a cosmopolitan public theology asks of us

Following Kang, and extending her with Appiah and Derrida, I argue that a cosmopolitan public theology makes three demands on a publication like this one. First, it must hold particular traditions and planetary obligation together rather than trading one for the other; the writer who speaks only from the tradition is parochial, and the writer who speaks from nowhere is a tourist. Second, it must move interreligious work past tolerance toward mutual critique, which means letting the Muslim jurist name the Christian history of sin and letting the Christian name her own. Third, it must count empire, including the empire that still sits in the law of the land where most of us read these words.

Kang ends with a line from Paul Celan: *Ich bin du, wenn ich ich bin* (I am you, when I am I). The chiasmus is the whole argument. I become capable of you only when I am most fully myself, and I am most fully myself only in the crossing. A public theology worth the name will carry no passport, and it will still know where it is from.
