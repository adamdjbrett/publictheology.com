## ***Collaborative Labs with a Long View***

The University of Michigan opened meaningful avenues for learning together in designing “History Labs.” Cook and Chin (2024, 388-389) describe this as a “pedagogical model that is fully collaborative, project centered, and divided into component parts pursued by multiple teams of research students (each one taking on a different part of the project as a whole).” These labs allow students to choose and focus their studies in partnership with other students, faculty, and community partners local and global. Collaborative at its foundation and grounded in peer review, students are held accountable in presenting their work for faculty and partners across their lab. Labs diverse as *Policing and Social Justice, Living and Dying in Late-Medieval London,* and *Collaborative Holocaust Research* are ongoing, offering students opportunities to be part of lasting work in their projects. This cumulative potential holds benefits for both the fields of study and also for students in subsequent semesters. It’s a design with a long view for collaborative learning and community knowledge production.

## ***Collaborative Commitment with a Long View***

Though somewhat hesitant toward aspects of public facing work, my understanding has shifted as I have begun writing publicly about the need for more generative and collaborative work across the diverse Body of Christ. I am confident that our work together can be more faithful and fruitful with the long view of collaborative learning in the History Labs model. And my own faithful participation begins with a public commitment to that collaboration on my part.

My classmates and instructors are pushing me to be a better pastor and student teacher across the wider Body of Christ. Sharing our semester as a “Public Theology Lab,” they keep me grounded and surrounded with rigor, challenging encouragement, and accountability. I want my work to answer affirmatively to the great question from [James Jones, Jr.](https://jejones.hcommons.org) (2026): “Could the people whose lives inform my scholarship find it, understand it, and see something of their own experience in it?” [Daryl Washington](https://dwashington62.hcommons.org/about/) (2026) inspires me to keep both academic work and church life "less as a fixed location and more as a public practice of learning and collaboration.” These public theologians are pushing me to remain authentically grounded and accountable in my community of faith as I grow and practice, but also to give *and to receive* the great artistry, intellect, and wisdom that always come as gifts through serving and learning with my neighbors in local, real-time ecumenical service. Like healing arts for the Body, this local portion of “public facing work” with my neighbors on the ground makes me more courageous for the long view with collaborative work.



&nbsp;

## ***Collaborative “Can Do”***

We’re far from the generative unity in our diversity that Jesus prayed for us, “that they may all be one” (John 17:21). But [Willie James Jennings](https://divinity.yale.edu/profile/willie-james-jennings)' significant portfolio consistently demonstrates there’s a most important place to begin. Jennings had a student ask, “How can we acknowledge and celebrate race beyond skin color, that is, how can we celebrate ethnic diversity without focusing on race as color?” Jennings replied,

> *[C]elebration begins by learning histories and knowing stories … to live with those who tell them and to be a part of their lives… The goal is that you know my story. Learn my story. Cherish my story. Remember my story. Never take it from me. Never steal it from me, but learn to live in it with me and together be schooled by it. And I will do the same for you* (Jennings,  2019, 44:41-46:21).



Jennings’ guidance gives us more healing art. That’s the way I want my ecumenical work to read and to play out in action.
