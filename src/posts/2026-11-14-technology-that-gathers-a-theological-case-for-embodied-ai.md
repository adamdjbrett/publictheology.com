AI has inundated our news feeds and social posts. Around every corner in the digital world, we find AI ads, website designs, software, blogs, and anywhere else content is produced. For a tool that draws us further into digital spaces, there’s much to wonder about concerning its value for human flourishing. Since the introduction of the iPhone in 2007,[^1] we’ve been living in a technological world that’s more “you”-shaped than “us”-shaped. With the advent of consumer AI worldwide, we’ve been increasingly pulled into social interaction with machines, sometimes preferring them over our human counterparts. With no slowdown in sight, I’d like to suggest that a potential reversal of personalized technology back toward communal technology lies on the horizon. I believe that introducing AI into physical tech (physical AI), and specifically embodied AI, can lead back toward a communal and thereby healthier relationship with technology.

For years, technology sought to produce and sell to us a greater sense of enchantment and otherworldly experiences. There were color television and VCRs in the 1970s, laser tag and the camcorder in the 1980s, Furby and Bop It in the 1990s, and then came the 2000s. Prior to this, much of our technology was shared in the home. Albert Borgmann traces this shift in technology from the communal to the personal in modern Western history, and the smartphone carries it further than anything before it.[^2] Although there were devices like the Atari and Game Boy, even these experiences had limits on how personal they could be, with communal elements built in. You could connect Game Boys together and do things like trade and battle Pokémon. And of course, with Atari, games happened on the single screen a household typically had that was set up in a shared living space. It wasn’t until the rise of the smartphone that technology devolved into a personalized, portable, tailor-made experience that drove people out of the living room watching the same movie and into their rooms watching their own YouTube videos and scrolling through their own feeds. We are now seeing the psychological impact of this ever-increasing screen time.[^3]

When generative AI was released to the public in 2022,[^4] things didn’t get better. In fact, users were pulled deeper into their phones, with new AI chatbots like ChatGPT that can exploit users’ loneliness. Because these systems were trained on a huge swath of the human corpus, they’re able to take on convincing human personas. There are some companies that capitalize on this by allowing the user to craft human avatars like Replika and Character.AI. What’s more, users increasingly consult their AI chatbot for advice and even mental health counseling, and in one survey of AI users with mental health conditions, more than a third rated it as more beneficial than human therapy.[^5] And now we’re left wondering: How could the evolution of technology be put on a better path? And how could a theological reflection recenter us in our thinking about these rapidly evolving tools?

Theology, and Christian theology in particular, provides us with an interesting ethical framework through which we can begin to reorient ourselves in our relationships with technology. A distinctive of the Christian faith is found in the belief that God is mysteriously three-in-one: God is triune. And how we most clearly see God is through Jesus, God incarnate, who is the perfect image of the invisible God (Col. 1:15; Heb. 1:3).[^6] The inherent nature of God, then, is communal, and God’s self-revelation is incarnate. As creatures who reflect the image of God, we, too, are called to be in relationship (communal) and present with (incarnate) the rest of God’s creation. This doesn’t just inform our relationships with God and people, but it also shapes how we relate to inanimate creation produced by God and by ourselves as well. In Genesis, one of the first commands given to humans was to govern and be good stewards of creation: to “fill the earth, and subdue it; and rule over” every living thing (Gen. 1:28). We are also called to be co-creators with Christ (1 Cor. 3:9).[^7] The creator-creature distinction is apparent between us and God, but there’s another layer that exists between us and our own creation. As God, in Christ, was incarnate with his own creation, so we too must work out what it means to be incarnate with our own creations.

And so what does an incarnational, communal technology look like? I believe the clearest expression of this in AI can be found in embodied AI. By embodied AI, I mean AI that lives in a body you share a room with, not an app that follows you from screen to screen. There isn’t a single form factor this kind of tech requires. It does need to take on a physical form (incarnational) and have a gathering effect versus a scattering effect (communal). This is most visible in AI robot companions (a form of embodied AI). They’re inherently incarnational. There’s a physicality to them that’s limiting and that doesn’t give you the sense that they travel around with you everywhere you go or are transferable between other tech like laptops and phones. And I would also argue that there’s a gathering effect they have over and above personalized, screen technology. As with Bop It and Furby, embodied AI has within it the potential to reverse course and bring us back to a shared experience with technology. One study found that when a robot on a team made vulnerable statements, such as admitting its own mistakes, the humans talked with one another about twice as much, spread the conversation somewhat more evenly, and rated their group more positively.[^8]

More studies like this should be done to find out whether this effect is as prominent as this study or our own intuition might have us think. Regardless of such work, Christian theology prompts us to think in the categories of community, incarnation, and the creator-creature distinction. AI robot companions are the kinds of devices that lend themselves naturally to such categories. Much like pets, which is how many AI robot companions are marketed (for example, Sony’s aibo, KEYi Tech’s Loona, and Casio’s Moflin),[^9] the communal element can be baked in and the AI has become embodied. As its creators, how will we now govern and be good stewards of this technology that has the ability to recalibrate our relationship with AI?
