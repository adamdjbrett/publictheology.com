## **Name them**

All Saints Sunday helps us stop and name the many who have gone before us, some working in faith despite their station and situation. It’s a day to tell the stories of not only famous martyrs like Stephen calling down blessings on his tormentors as they stoned him (Acts 7:59-60) but also to remember and name lesser known souls that have served in our systems of slavery and hierarchy. “Some of these people are known only by their occupation and a date… such as Molly, her son Tom Mask, and her daughters Phillis, Belindia, Jenny, and Elizabeth.”[^1] Long hidden from history’s eyes, these persons were among the many slaves used to the benefit of the College of William & Mary in Williamsburg, Virginia since its charter in 1693. Scattered and scant records sometimes expose how these individuals were assessed like tools. "A List of Negroes at College” on yellowed parchment shows “Dick was described as ‘almost an invalid.’ Old Kate, Old Lucy, and Nanny were also labeled ‘invalid,’ and Effy was considered “not much better.”[^2]



[The Lemon Project](https://www.wm.edu/sites/lemonproject/) is named for Lemon, a man once enslaved by William & Mary. The project’s “unearthing authentic narratives of slavery and its legacies at William and Mary is vital to community healing.”[^3] By “sharing authority,” the project provides “a platform for descendent groups to share their community histories to broad audiences.”[^4] Similarly, All Saints is an ongoing commemorative ritual, where churches join in faith with descendant voices to name our ancestors, our uses and misuses of one another, and especially to tell “[t]heir stories… and give breadth to rightly interpreting the past in a way that offers insight for building more equitable futures.”[^5]



## **Learn from them**

> <em>Africana DH claims African and ancestral communicative technology as it's true early beginnings, where healing, recovery, and fulfillment of purpose was its primary role as opposed to industrial, commercial, and military technology uses as found in the west (Somé, 61). African and central communicative technology recognizes the role of spirit as a technological concept while also informing technology.[^6]</em>

Making a place for voices from different communities in my research has me reconsidering my habits and perspective about the broken Body of Christ. I have grown accustomed to naming the splits, the exponential divisions and separations across history where communities of Christians leave other Christians to begin their own churches or denominations. It’s become easy to pontificate about the splintering and caricature “them” and them and them.

It is relatively easy to find archival materials and statements about why and when this or that Christian denomination left “them.” But William and Mary’s yellow parchment helps me rethink how documents, no matter how formal or informal, are only a snapshot of our local lives in time. We are a changing world, learning and growing through our experiences and the struggles of those who have gone before us. Africana DH challenges all to trust that we can be challenged and changed by “them,” the very people we have had conflict with or from whom we have separated ourselves. It “challenges traditional DH by affirming the role of spirit as a necessary, indigenous technology that moves through time and space, allowing the user to experience narration and new meaningful ways.”[^7]

## **Trust the Spirit**

Descendants of these saints are sharing their story and helping us all live a better story together. On All Saints Sunday, from our cities and towns to our sciences and traditions of faith, we can name that, name *them*—those who have taught us about ourselves and, in turn, keep us living into a better story together.
